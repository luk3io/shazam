How Shazam Works

1What Shazam does

Hold a phone up in a noisy room for a few seconds, and Shazam tells you the name of the song playing. That's the whole product. The hard part is everything hidden underneath it.

The core problem is a search problem with brutal constraints: take a short, noisy audio clip — maybe 10 seconds, picked up through a cheap microphone, mixed with room echo, chatter, and whatever else is in the environment — and find its exact match among a database of millions of songs, in under a second, with essentially no false positives. Comparing raw waveforms directly doesn't work: the same song recorded twice in different rooms produces two very different lists of numbers, even though a human ear recognises them instantly. Shazam needs a representation of a song that survives background noise, distortion, and a bad microphone, while still being unique enough to distinguish one track from millions of others. It's a bit like recognising one person's voice in a stadium crowd, when you've only ever heard them speak once, briefly, on the phone.

Shazam's approach to this — the audio fingerprinting technique this page walks through — was developed around 1999, before smartphones existed. The original service ran over the phone network: you'd dial 2580, hold your phone up to the music for a few seconds, hang up, and a text message would arrive with the song's name. It later became one of the first breakout mobile apps, and Apple acquired the company in 2018 for roughly $400 million. The underlying algorithm — the one this demo reproduces — is largely the same idea that made the 2580 dial-in work: turn audio into a sparse set of landmark points, hash those points, and vote.

2The pipeline at a glance

Six stages turn a raw recording into a confident match. Each one strips away information the next stage doesn't need, until only a small, robust, searchable signature is left.

  1. Waveform— the microphone captures raw air pressure over time.
  2. Fourier transform— a short slice of that waveform is decomposed into the sine waves (frequencies) it's made of.
  3. Spectrogram— repeat the transform across many overlapping slices and stack the results into a time × frequency picture.
  4. Peak detection— keep only the strongest, most distinctive points in that picture — a sparse "constellation".
  5. Fingerprints— pair nearby peaks together and hash each pair into a compact, searchable number.
  6. Matching— look up those hashes in a song database and let matching hashes "vote" for the correct song.

3Each stage explained

The same six stages, now with the reasoning and the analogies used in the live demo.

1Waveform

A microphone converts sound into a single long list of numbers: air pressure sampled many thousand times per second. It's a completely accurate record of the sound, but it's also close to useless for recognition on its own — two recordings of the same song, made a few milliseconds apart or through different microphones, produce wildly different number sequences even though they sound identical. What actually identifies a song isn't the raw pressure values; it's the pattern of musical frequencies layered on top of each other over time. Pulling those frequencies out is the job of the next stage.

2Fourier transform

The Fourier transform takes a signal that mixes many frequencies together and breaks it back apart into the individual sine waves that were added up to make it — along with the strength (amplitude) of each one.

Analogy — the coin-sorting machine

Imagine a piggy bank full of mixed coins, tipped out into one pile. You can't tell how much money you have just by looking at the pile. Run it through a coin-sorting machine and it separates the pile into stacks by denomination, each stack telling you a coin type and how many of them there are. The Fourier transform does the same thing to sound: it takes one jumbled pressure signal and sorts it into a set of "denominations" — frequencies — each with an amplitude (how much of that frequency is present).

Shazam (and this demo) uses the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT): the same mathematical result as a naive Fourier transform, but computed in O(N log N) time instead of O(N²) — the difference between "runs instantly on a phone" and "too slow to be usable".

A single Fourier transform over an entire song would only tell you which frequencies occur somewhere in the whole track, with no sense of when. To recover timing, the transform is applied to many short, overlapping slices of audio instead — each only tens of milliseconds long — which is exactly what the next stage does.

3Spectrogram

Running a short-window Fourier transform (a Short-Time Fourier Transform, or STFT) repeatedly across the recording, one overlapping slice at a time, and stacking the results side by side produces a spectrogram: a 2D picture where the x-axis is time, the y-axis is frequency, and brightness is amplitude. It shows exactly which frequencies were present, and precisely when.

4Peak detection / constellation map

A spectrogram still contains almost everything — background noise, room reverb, every faint overtone. Most of that detail is fragile: it changes between two recordings of the same song. So Shazam sparsifies the spectrogram, keeping only the points that are the strongest local peak in a small neighbourhood of time and frequency around them, and discarding everything else.

Analogy — back to the piggy bank

This is like tipping the sorted coin stacks out again and sweeping away all the low-value coins, keeping only the handful of largest ones. You lose most of the "mass" of the original pile, but the big coins you kept are the ones least likely to be missing next time you count — they're the coins that survive a sloppy recount. In audio terms: peaks are the parts of the spectrogram most likely to survive background noise, compression, and EQ changes, because they're the loudest, most dominant features at that instant.

Once only the peaks remain, even their loudness (amplitude) is discarded — only their coordinates (time, frequency) matter from here on. What's left is a sparse scatter of points often called the constellation map, because it looks like a star field laid over the original spectrogram.

5Fingerprints

A single peak, on its own, isn't distinctive enough — plenty of songs share an isolated frequency at some point in time. So Shazam combines peaks into pairs. Each peak in turn acts as an anchor, and is paired with other peaks that occur shortly afterwards, within a bounded "target zone" of time and frequency. Each pair is reduced to three numbers — the anchor's frequency, the paired peak's frequency, and the time gap between them — written as (f₁, f₂, Δt) — which are packed together into a single compact hash (a 32-bit integer in this implementation).

Analogy — the library shelf

Think of every possible hash value as one shelf in an enormous library. Every song that happens to contain that exact (f₁, f₂, Δt) pattern gets a book placed on that shelf, with a note inside recording which song it is and exactly where in the song that pattern occurred. Later, given an unknown clip, you compute its own hashes, walk over to the matching shelves, and see which books keep turning up.

Pairing peaks this way is what makes the fingerprint robust: even if noise wipes out or adds a few isolated peaks, plenty of the original pairs — and therefore plenty of the original hashes — survive intact.

6Matching (offset voting)

To identify a clip, Shazam computes its fingerprints exactly as above, then looks each hash up in the song database. Every hit records which song it came from and how far into that song it occurred. For every hit, Shazam computes an offset — offset = time in song − time in recording — and casts one vote for the pair (song, offset).

If the clip really is a piece of a particular song, then almost all of its hashes align to the same point in that song, so nearly every vote for the correct song lands on the same offset — producing one large, sharp spike of votes. Coincidental hash collisions with wrong songs, by contrast, land on essentially random offsets and spread thinly across many buckets. The song with the tallest single-offset spike — and how tall that spike is — becomes the match and its confidence score.

4How the code is organised

The demo is hand-written vanilla JavaScript with no external libraries — including the FFT. Each source file has one job. src/dsp.js is written to run unmodified in Node (for tests), as a browser <script>, and inside a Web Worker.

Source files and their responsibilities
FileResponsibilityKey exports / functions
src/dsp.js The pure DSP core — no DOM, no Web Audio, just math on arrays. This is the whole algorithm described in Section 3. CONFIG, fft, hann, stft, findPeaks, fingerprint, buildDatabase, matchQuery
src/audio.js Browser audio I/O: decoding embedded MP3s to mono PCM, slicing clips, warming up and streaming microphone capture, evening out the recording level, and playback. Web Audio only runs on the main thread, so all of this lives here rather than in the worker. src/worker.js The Web Worker message handler. Runs the CPU-heavy fingerprinting (many FFTs) off the main thread so the UI never stalls. The build prepends dsp.js to this file so self.DSP exists inside the worker. self.onmessage → { id, fingerprints }
src/renderers.js One Canvas 2D renderer per pipeline stage. Each renderer takes precomputed stage data plus an animation progress value (0–1) and draws that stage's visualisation. Renderers.waveform, fourier, spectrogram, peaks, fingerprints, matching
src/controller.js The app's state machine: loads the song database in the background via the worker, handles mode selection (enable-mic → live record / canned), precomputes every stage's data up front, drives the animation and playback clocks, and wires up keyboard/button controls. Audio is downsampled to 11025 Hz before analysis — plenty of resolution for fingerprinting, and cheap to compute on. Each analysis frame is 1024 samples, about 93 ms, with a 512-sample hop between frames — 50% overlap between consecutive windows: 

function stft(pcm, cfg = CONFIG) {
  const { frameSize, hopSize } = cfg;
  const win = hann(frameSize), numBins = frameSize / 2;
  const numFrames = Math.max(0, 1 + Math.floor((pcm.length - frameSize) / hopSize));
  const columns = [];
  const re = new Float64Array(frameSize), im = new Float64Array(frameSize);
  for (let f = 0; f < numFrames; f++) {
    const off = f * hopSize;
    for (let i = 0; i < frameSize; i++) { re[i] = pcm[off + i] * win[i]; im[i] = 0; }
    fft(re, im);
    const col = new Float32Array(numBins);
    for (let b = 0; b < numBins; b++) col[b] = Math.hypot(re[b], im[b]);
    columns.push(col);
  }
  return { columns, numFrames, numBins };
}

The fingerprint hash itself packs the anchor frequency bin, the paired peak's frequency bin, and the time gap between them into one unsigned 32-bit integer: 

const hash = (((a.f & 0x1FF) << 23) | ((b.f & 0x1FF) << 14) | (dt & 0x3FFF)) >>> 0;

9 bits for each frequency bin plus 14 bits for the time delta adds up to exactly 32 bits — the whole fingerprint fits in a single JavaScript unsigned integer, cheap to store and to use as a hash-map key.

5How to build & run

Build

  1. Put MP3s in ./songs/, named Artist - Song title.mp3 (5–10 songs recommended — full songs are embedded, so more songs means a bigger file and a slower first load).
  2. Run npm run build. This produces shazam-demo.html at the repository root and prints the song count and resulting file size.

Run the demo

  1. Double-click shazam-demo.html — it opens directly in your browser, fully offline. No server, no install, no command line.
  2. Use Chrome or Edge for the live-mic path. Safari often blocks microphone access on file:// pages; canned playback works in any browser.
  3. Wait for the start screen to show "Ready ✓" — songs are fingerprinted in the background as soon as the page opens, so this is usually done before you need it.
  4. Pick a mode: Canned playback — choose a song from the dropdown and click ▶. Live — click 🎤 Enable microphone and, once it reads "Mic ready ✓", click ● Record & identify and play the song into the microphone for about 10 seconds; the waveform draws in real time as it records. (Enabling the mic as its own step lets the microphone settle first, so the start of the recording isn't lost to warm-up.)
  5. Step through the six stages with Spacebar (or the Next button); ← goes back, R replays the current stage's animation, and the fullscreen button (⛶) is there for the projector.
  6. On the waveform and Fourier stages, ▶ Play clip plays your recording back — on the Fourier stage the highlighted slice follows the playhead, so its frequency breakdown updates in time with what you hear.
Note: this documentation page is independent of shazam-demo.html and the slide deck — it doesn't need a build, mic access, or any of the embedded songs. It's meant to be read on its own, before or after the live demo.

Rehearsal checklist

  • Click Enable microphone and grant permission (browsers re-ask on every file:// load), then wait for "Mic ready ✓" before recording.
  • Confirm a real live recording of a database song lands the correct match, in the room where you'll present.
  • Have canned playback ready as the instant fallback if the live match fails.
  • Run npm test to confirm the DSP core still passes (FFT, peak detection, fingerprint determinism, end-to-end match).
  • If you swap songs, re-run npm run build and re-check both the build's printed file size and a fresh match.
